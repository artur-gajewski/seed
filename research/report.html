SEED RESEARCH · CPU + MPS STUDY · THREE PAIRED SEEDS

Which small model learns more efficiently?

MPS replication and CPU comparison

All eight training jobs were repeated on MPS using exact copies of the CPU corpora, identical initial seeds, model sizes, 800-update budgets, and optimizer settings. CPU results remain preserved. No CPU fallback environment variable was enabled.

Device result

At the original parameter scale, MPS completed the Transformer update budget 2.23× faster and the GRU budget 1.96× faster than CPU. These single-seed scale results need replication. The small-model device comparison below shows that GPU acceleration is workload-dependent. The small models and original-scale GRU remain close to the 14.3% constant-color baseline. The original-scale MPS-trained Transformer is an exception, examined below. Faster training alone is not evidence of better reasoning or lower electrical energy.

Recommendation for Seed

Use the Transformer on MPS as the default for training at roughly 3.3 million parameters. It trained faster and produced the strongest task result in this experiment.

  • Normal training: Transformer + MPS.
  • Small, quick experiments: CPU—the GPU offered little benefit at that scale.
  • GRU: Keep as a research baseline. Its lower prose loss did not translate into reliable color retrieval. CPU was faster for GRU generation.
  • Quantization and pruning: Keep optional. Our int8 implementation reduced storage but slowed decoding; pruning damaged quality.

Before treating the 88.5% accuracy as dependable, repeat the larger Transformer experiment with seeds 43 and 44 on both devices. That is the most useful next experiment.

These recommendations apply to the tested 128-byte context. Seed’s usual 256-byte context still needs benchmarking.

Unexpected Transformer accuracy improvement: cross-device check

The original-scale MPS-trained Transformer achieved 88.5% exact-answer accuracy versus 13.2% for the CPU-trained seed-42 model, despite similar prompt-inclusive losses. Each saved checkpoint was then evaluated on both CPU and MPS. The accuracy follows the trained weights: moving either checkpoint to the other device preserves its score. This rules out an inference-device explanation for the observed score difference in these evaluations. Training numerics can lead to different optimization trajectories, but this experiment does not isolate the mechanism or establish that MPS generally learns better. This is one paired seed; additional initializations are needed.

Numerical differences between CPU and MPS are expected; identical seeds do not promise identical training trajectories. A low prose or prompt-inclusive task loss still does not demonstrate reliable answer retrieval.

MPS memory and limitations

Peak RSS is process host memory, not peak GPU memory. The MPS runs additionally record live tensor allocations and driver allocations after benchmarking; those are snapshots, not peaks. Driver allocations can include cached buffers and framework allocations. No watts or joules were measured. CPU and GPU runs were conducted sequentially; host activity and thermal state were not fully controlled.
